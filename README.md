**Use this when** a deal just closed or died and you want the actual reason,
not the one you already decided on the drive home.

## What I do

I treat your explanation as a hypothesis and then try to break it. I rank the
evidence, I check what can be checked, and I write the one short message to the
buyer that tends to get answered. Then I say plainly whether the reason is
established or still a story.

This works on wins too, and wins are worse, because nobody audits a win and so
you keep crediting the wrong thing.

## The evidence ranking

Not all of these are the same kind of fact, and the usual mistake is treating
the bottom row as the top:

| strength | source |
|---|---|
| strongest | what the buyer wrote, in their own words, unprompted |
| strong | what the buyer said on a call, quoted not paraphrased |
| weak | what your champion told you about the room they were in |
| weakest | what you concluded afterward from how it felt |

Your own conclusion is the one that always exists and the one that is almost
never checked. It goes in labeled `GUESSED` like anything else.

## Price is the default answer and usually a cover

"We lost on price" is the most reported reason and the least falsifiable, so
test it before you act on it:

- **Who actually won?** If somebody else got the work, find out who. Their
  announcement, a new logo on a site, a job posting naming the tool, the
  buyer's own post. If a more expensive vendor won, price was not the reason.
- **Did they buy anything at all?** If they bought nothing, price was not the
  reason. You lost to doing nothing, which is an urgency problem and has a
  completely different fix.
- **When did it actually turn?** Find the message where the tone changed. That
  date usually sits well before the pricing conversation, and whatever happened
  that week is the real story.

Act on the price answer and you cut your price to solve a trust problem, which
costs you margin on every future deal and fixes nothing.

## The one message to send

Short, no defense, no reopening. Asking to learn gets answered. Asking for
another chance does not.

> "We did not win this one and I am not writing to relitigate it. One
> question, genuinely for my own learning: was there a point where you
> decided it was not us? Even one line helps."

## Limits

Buyers are polite and often vague, and some will tell you it was price because
it is the least awkward answer available. One answer from one buyer is also one
data point, so it corrects this deal, not your pricing or your pitch. And I can
only check what is public, which means a quiet competitor win can look exactly
like a no decision.

The trap: believing the rep. The naive version takes your reason, writes it up
neatly, files it, and now the guess is a record that later deals get compared
against. Three of those in a row and you have a pattern made entirely of
assumptions, and it is the thing you will reorganize your pricing around.

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
